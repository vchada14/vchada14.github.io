Broke — 1

Half a million rows per click

A click-through to the postings behind each number filtered the entire table. It blew the memory limit — the query wouldn't run at all.

Stopped calculating at query time and precomputed the lists instead. Same answers, but instant.

Broke — 2

A rolling seven-day metric went blank

No error, nothing — just an empty cell where a number should be. The marts refresh overnight in batch, and when a refresh slipped past seven days, the whole window ended up sitting after the last data point.

My first instinct was to make it visible — an “as of” stamp, and a dynamic date label so what it says can't drift from what it's actually calculating. But that just tells you the number is stale. It doesn't stop it going blank. So I changed what the window anchors to: instead of counting back from today, it counts back from the refresh date.

02 — Owning operations

Cargo storage planning, Menzies Aviation

They mainly used two systems

WMS and SITA

The warehouse management system tracked where cargo physically sat and for how long. SITA carried the flight-level detail — commodity type, handling requirements. Neither was much use alone: WMS could tell you a shipment occupied a bay for eleven hours but not what was in it; SITA could tell you it was perishable without saying where it went.

So nobody could answer the question that actually mattered — which cargo types consume the most space, and for how long.

Why it matters

A warehouse isn't uniform

Cold storage, racking and open floor are different resources, and a tonne of pharma doesn't need the same footprint as a tonne of general cargo.

What I built

Volume and dwell time by cargo category

Combined six months of data across both systems. How much of each type came through, and how long it stayed — measured from arrival scan to release scan.

Try it — why volume alone misleads

A high-volume category that clears fast needs a different allocation from a low-volume one that sits for days

Sort by tonnage and you get one ranking. Sort by space consumed over time — volume × dwell — and it inverts.

Illustrative figures — shown to demonstrate the principle
Outcome

That went to the industrial engineers

They used it for efficient storage allocation by cargo type.

03 — Responsible for other people's work

University-wide ELR approval workstream

Starting condition

Eight colleges, eight data managers, nobody formally leading

Every college handled experiential learning record approvals differently — that's the record created when a student accepts a co-op placement. We were all at the same level, so I took it on informally.

01

Wrote down how each college actually works

We'd all been describing our own process and assuming everyone else matched. Once documented, the differences were obvious and we stopped talking past each other.

02

Structured the sessions, then looked outward

An agenda so each manager had airtime for what they knew about their own college. And I went outside the group early, to Admin Application Development Services.

03

Now and long-term, side by side

Some wanted the full redesign, others something shippable. Two columns meant nobody's idea got thrown out — the ambitious ones became the roadmap.

Try it — sort the group's ideas

Every idea had to land somewhere

Some people wanted the full redesign, others wanted something shippable this term. Two columns meant the argument didn't need settling — it needed sequencing.

Outcome

Taken forward by DATOM leadership

Being built into NUworks staff utilities now. That deploys next semester.
